# Research review: the intermediate plateau, output, chunks, shadowing and fluency measurement

*September 2026. Verification pass over the research underpinning the planned
plateau-busting features (chunk drilling, pushed-output practice, self-recorded
monologues, shadowing), done before any data model or implementation. Each
claim was checked against primary sources — journal articles, the original
PDFs, official government pages — rather than blog summaries. Five of the core
documents were read in full: Richards (2008), Pawley & Syder (1983), Ellis
(2012), Krashen (1998), Schmidt (2010), plus the Oxford thesis underlying
Whitworth & Rose (2025) and the British Council Aptis fluency report.*

**Confidence labels used throughout:** `confirmed` (primary source read, claim
verified), `supported-but-nuanced` (real evidence, with caveats that change
how we should use it), `contested` (credible sources disagree), `unverified`
(no direct evidence exists — inference only), `gap` (searched explicitly,
nothing found).

---

## 1. The intermediate plateau (Richards 2008)

**Verified:** Richards, *Moving Beyond the Plateau* (Cambridge University
Press, 2008) is exactly what it looks like — a teacher-facing **synthesis
booklet with no original data**, citing a small literature per chapter and
recommending Cambridge's own coursebooks in the back matter. Its five plateau
characteristics are confirmed verbatim:

1. gap between receptive and productive competence
2. fluency progressed at the expense of complexity
3. limited vocabulary range
4. production adequate but lacking natural (chunk-based) character
5. persistent fossilized errors

Full text (mirror): http://mikesclass.pbworks.com/f/Richards-Beyond-Plateau.pdf

**Two corrections to how we'd been quoting it:**

- The vocabulary figure is **3,000 words, not 2,000**. Richards: "Learners'
  vocabulary development is still at the 3,000-word level," with a ladder of
  5,000 to read unsimplified novels (this is actually **Hirsh & Nation 1992**
  — Richards' in-text "Hu and Nation (1992)" is a citation error) and
  5,000–6,000 receptive as the top-of-intermediate threshold (O'Keeffe,
  McCarthy & Carter 2007). These are **English text-coverage figures**;
  nothing licenses porting them to Arabic, where diglossia gives the dialect
  and MSA partly divergent lexicons.
- The plateau construct itself is **contested/thin as an empirical claim**.
  Richards' only direct evidence that the plateau exists is Fan Yi (2007), a
  non-peer-reviewed paper on a Chinese university website. The five
  characteristics are expert synthesis of real literatures (Skehan, Schmidt,
  Swain, Pawley & Syder), but "the intermediate plateau" as a measured
  longitudinal phenomenon has no strong study behind it. Treat it as a
  useful organizing frame, not established science — fine for product design,
  not for "research shows" marketing copy.

## 2. Output hypothesis (Swain) and noticing (Schmidt)

**The premise holds; the mechanism is shakier than the summaries say.**

- **"Comprehensible input alone is insufficient for native-like production"
  — supported-but-nuanced.** The Canadian French immersion data (Swain 1985;
  Harley & Swain 1984) is undisputed descriptively: ~7–8 years of immersion
  input produced near-native comprehension and clearly non-native production.
  Krashen (1998, *System* 26:175–182, read in full —
  https://www.sdkrashen.com/content/articles/comprehensible_output.pdf)
  disputes the diagnosis, not the data.
- **"Pushed output causes noticing-the-gap and drives acquisition" — mixed
  direct evidence.** Izumi (2002, SSLA) supports it for ESL relativization;
  Izumi & Bigelow (2000, TESOL Quarterly) found no unique output advantage;
  Shehadeh's neutral review (2002, *Language Learning* 52:597–647) concluded
  the output→acquisition link was under-evidenced after 15 years. Swain
  herself only ever claimed output "sometimes, under some conditions"
  facilitates learning (Swain & Lapkin 1995).
- **The one direct Arabic-L2 test is partially null.** Nassif (2019, *Foreign
  Language Annals* 52(2), n=80 beginning learners of Arabic —
  https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/flan.12387): **output did not
  promote noticing better than input**; form salience was what mattered.
  A mild caution against assuming the English-literature mechanism transfers.
- **Krashen's strongest point cuts the other way for an app.** His scarcity
  argument — naturally occurring pushed output is vanishingly rare (~1
  grammatical output modification per classroom hour) — is an argument
  *against classrooms relying on it arising naturally*, and therefore an
  argument *for* a product that deliberately engineers output tasks. The
  honest claim for Hakiya: output practice creates opportunities the input
  diet doesn't, and interaction as a package robustly works (Mackey & Goo
  2007 meta-analysis, pooled d ≈ 1.09) — not "science proves speaking
  practice causes acquisition."
- **Noticing (Schmidt 1990): use the weak version only.** Schmidt himself
  (2010, read in full) retreated to "people learn about the things they
  attend to" and conceded the strong form is untestable; Truscott (1998) is
  the standing critique. The weak version is mainstream and underwrites
  focus-on-form design (highlighting gaps, feedback) — that's all we need.

## 3. Chunks and formulaic language (Pawley & Syder, Ellis)

**The core product bet survives verification; two specific numbers don't.**

- **Confirmed (primary texts read):** Pawley & Syder (1983,
  https://lextutor.ca/rt/pawley_syder_83.pdf) claims what we thought:
  fluent speech is built from memorized/lexicalized multi-word units; long
  pause-free runs in native speech are overwhelmingly formulaic. Modern
  psycholinguistics vindicates the storage claim on evidence they didn't
  have: formulaic sequences are read faster by natives *and* L2 learners
  (Conklin & Schmitt 2008), and phrase frequency effects exist for fully
  compositional four-word strings (Arnon & Snider 2010).
- **"Hundreds of thousands of sentence stems" — unverified.** It is P&S's
  own explicitly hedged order-of-magnitude hypothesis, never counted. Cite
  it as their estimate or not at all.
- **"One clause at a time" — contested in strong form.** Pauses do cluster
  at clause boundaries (that observation stands, and is the basis of the
  fluency metrics in §5), but production planning is flexibly incremental
  and often sub-clausal (Konopka 2012; Martin et al.).
- **The "phrasal teddy bear" (Ellis 2012, *ARAL* 32:17–44, read in full —
  https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/nickellis-new/wp-content/uploads/sites/1284/2021/07/ARAL2012offprint.pdf)
  is confirmed**, explicitly extending Hasselgren's (1994) "lexical teddy
  bears": learners overuse a few safe high-frequency phrases and underuse
  the broad native formulaic repertoire. Also relevant: advanced L2
  processing is driven by raw frequency where native processing is driven by
  coherence/MI (Ellis, Simpson-Vlach & Maynard 2008) — i.e. learners need
  deliberate exposure to strong-association chunks that raw frequency alone
  won't surface.
- **Does explicit chunk instruction work? Supported-but-nuanced.** Chunk
  counts in speech robustly correlate with rated fluency/proficiency
  (Boers et al. 2006; Stengers et al. 2011; Yan 2020). The *causal*
  intervention evidence is thin: Boers et al. (2006) is n=32 with a
  perceived-proficiency outcome and is under a formal replication call
  (Thomson, Boers & Coxhead 2019). Awareness-raising alone produced limited
  uptake; the better-evidenced levers are **repeated exposure plus deliberate
  attention to specific sequences** — which fits an SRS-driven chunk deck
  better than a "notice the chunks" lesson.
- **Arabic: gap confirmed.** The only corpus-informed Arabic formulaic
  sequence list is MSA-oriented (Alghamdi & Atwell 2019, *IJCL* 24(2));
  nothing exists for Gulf/Egyptian/Yemeni dialect chunks. Any chunk
  inventory for Hakiya must be derived from dialect corpora + native review
  (the transcript-review pipeline is the natural source), not lifted from
  published pedagogical lists.

## 4. Shadowing (Whitworth & Rose 2025)

**The systematic review is real and checks out** — Whitworth & Rose (2025),
*Research Synthesis in Applied Linguistics* 1(2):239–269, 44 studies,
PRISMA-guided narrative synthesis (no meta-analysis, no pooled effect sizes).
Verified against the full open-access Oxford thesis it derives from:
https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:3104cae1-3a6b-400a-b173-de44384238d2

What it actually supports, by outcome:

| Outcome | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Fluency | Strongest signal — all 8 fluency studies positive (mostly speech-rate measures) |
| Comprehensibility | Positive (7/9 studies incl. both high-quality ones) |
| Prosody/suprasegmentals | Generally positive, mixed |
| Segmental accuracy | **Inconclusive** (4 studies, the 2 positive ones low quality) |
| Accentedness | **Contested** — the best study (Foote & McDonough 2017) found no gain |
| Listening (bottom-up) | Supported from a separate literature (Hamada), strongest for lower-proficiency learners |
| Durability | **Unverified** — 1 delayed post-test in 44 studies |

**Product implications:** frame shadowing feedback around fluency, prosody
and comprehensibility — *not* per-phoneme accuracy (which independently
matches the existing `shadowScoring.ts` rationale). Dosage parameters worth
adopting as defaults, held loosely (individual studies, no moderator
analysis): **~5 repetitions of one passage before plateau** (Shiki et al.
2010), 10–15 min sessions, 3–4×/week, 4–8 week programs; shadow without the
script first; learner-chosen material helps with the boredom problem.

**Arabic: gap confirmed.** Zero dialectal-Arabic shadowing studies exist (the
review's L2s: English 27, Japanese 5, French 1). The mechanism is
phonological and plausibly language-general, but pharyngeals/emphatics and
dialect prosody have no shadowing data at all — we are operating ahead of the
evidence here and shouldn't imply otherwise.

## 5. Fluency measurement and the monologue feature

**Framework confirmed:** Segalowitz (2010) — cognitive vs. utterance vs.
perceived fluency — is the standard taxonomy. The monologue feature measures
utterance fluency as a proxy for cognitive fluency, which is the framework's
intended use.

**Which measures carry signal (confirmed from the Aptis primary report,
Tavakoli, Nakatsuhara & Hunter 2017/2020 —
https://www.britishcouncil.org/sites/default/files/tavakoli_et_al_layout.pdf):**

- **Speed measures** (speech rate, articulation rate, mean length of run)
  discriminate *adjacent* CEFR levels — the workhorse metrics.
- **Pause location beats pause duration.** Mid-clause pausing is the L2
  signature (Tavakoli 2011); mid-clause silent pause length ordered
  A2 > B1 > B2 > C1. Pause *duration* is heavily personal/L1 speaking style
  (r ≈ .76 with the same speaker's L1 pausing — De Jong et al. 2013/2015),
  so raw pause-length scores partly grade personality. If we ever get an L1
  baseline recording from the user, partialing it out measurably improves
  validity.
- **Repair measures are non-linear and unstable** in short samples — report
  them descriptively, don't score on them.
- Raters' perceived fluency is predicted almost entirely by pause + speed
  measures; repairs contribute little (Bosker et al. 2013).

**Sample length — the honest verdict: 3–5 minutes is an engineering
inference, not an evidenced optimum.** No study varies L2 sample length and
reports reliability curves. What the evidence actually shows:

- The discriminating findings above come from **30–120 s tasks** (Aptis);
  TOEFL scores 45–60 s responses; ~60 s of monologue already explains 60% of
  variance in rated adequacy (Handley & Wang 2023, primary PDF read).
- Reliability gains come more from **multiple tasks on different topics than
  from one longer sample** (Xi & Mollaun 2006; In'nami & Koizumi 2016 —
  task effects exceed rater effects).
- Rate measures stabilize fast; rare-event measures (repairs, long
  mid-clause pauses) need more speech — the best quantitative guide is the
  clinical analogy (Heilmann et al. 2010: 3-min samples hit α ≥ .80 where
  1-min didn't).
- **Low-proficiency speakers often cannot fill even 45–120 s** (the Aptis A2
  abandonment finding). A fixed 3–5 min requirement would produce
  abandonment and unstable measures exactly where our beginners sit.

**Design consequence:** scale the target with level — e.g. ~1 min at
A-levels, 2–3 min at B-levels, with "several shorter prompts on different
topics" preferred over one long ramble when we want a reliable composite.
3–5 min stays defensible for advanced free-form monologues, but nothing is
lost by not hard-coding it as the universal target.

**Arabic: near-total gap.** Trentman (2017, *System* — speech rate/MLR gains
plus Egyptian-dialect feature use during study abroad) is nearly the whole
L2-Arabic utterance-fluency literature. Baker-Smemoe et al. (2014) found the
fluency–proficiency relationship **differs by language** across five L2s
including Arabic — a direct caution against importing English-calibrated
thresholds. Pause-location coding for Arabic needs its own clause-boundary
conventions (AS-units are English-centric); nothing published exists. Plan to
calibrate thresholds internally from our own data rather than from the
literature.

## 6. Hours-to-proficiency (FSI) and the diglossia question

- **FSI numbers confirmed from the primary source** (official State Dept
  page, verified via the 2021–2025 state.gov mirror): Category IV
  "super-hard," **88 weeks / 2,200 class hours to ILR S-3/R-3** — for
  full-time, professionally taught, aptitude-selected diplomats, *including
  MSA reading proficiency*. The user's suspicion is right: every "2,200
  hours for Arabic" claim on the web traces back to this one source; there
  is no independent empirical replication.
- **Genuinely new find — the only institutional dialect-vs-MSA timeline
  anywhere:** DLI's own catalogs (primary PDFs read) run separate 64-week
  basic courses for MSA, Egyptian, Iraqi and Levantine (all Category IV),
  and price the **MSA→dialect "conversion" course at 16 weeks full-time
  (~500–600 class hours)** — roughly a quarter of the from-scratch course.
  It's a curriculum allocation, not a measured time-to-proficiency, but it's
  the best available anchor for "how much of the 2,200 hours is the dialect
  conversation part."
  https://www.dliflc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/04/DLICatalog2011_2012_NEW.pdf
- **A conversational-dialect goal is a smaller, unmeasured target** than
  S-3/R-3: a large fraction of FSI's hours buys MSA literacy that a
  dialect-conversation learner doesn't need. Honest app framing: "2,200
  hours is FSI's estimate for diplomat-grade proficiency including MSA
  reading; a spoken-dialect goal is a different, smaller, unmeasured target."
- **MSA-first vs. integrated/dialect-first:** no randomized head-to-head
  exists, but the convergent evidence — study-abroad access studies (Shiri
  2013; Trentman 2013: MSA-only learners can't handle everyday interaction
  and retreat to English), 25 years of integrated-program outcome data
  (Al-Batal ed. 2018, *Arabic as One Language*), Nassif (2021: learners
  code-switch MSA/dialect along native-like lines, not confusion), and the
  one controlled experiment (Huntley 2024 MSU dissertation, artificial
  "Mini-Arabii": novices acquire two registers from day 1 at minor accuracy
  cost) — points one way: **integration doesn't damage MSA, and MSA-only
  leaves a spoken-interaction gap with real costs. No study shows MSA-first
  producing better spoken outcomes.** Direction: medium-high confidence;
  effect sizes: unknown.
- **Cross-dialect transfer:** Trentman & Shiri (2020, *Critical
  Multilingualism Studies* 8(1)) is the citation for "learning one dialect
  helps you decode others" — comprehension tracks phonological/lexical
  distance, contact and attitudes. Directly relevant to a Gulf/Egyptian/
  Yemeni multi-dialect app. Nobody has measured transfer *in learners over
  time*.
- **Legitimacy note for assessment features:** ACTFL's Arabic OPI policy
  treats Arabic as one language on a colloquial↔MSA continuum and accepts
  production anywhere on it — a dialect-only speaker can be formally rated.

## 7. What is genuinely unresearched for our case

Searched explicitly; these are confirmed gaps, and none of them should be
papered over with "research shows" copy:

1. **Dialect-only acquisition** — the entire integration literature studies
   MSA+dialect together; nobody has studied learners of *only* a spoken
   dialect, i.e. Hakiya's core user.
2. **Gulf/Khaliji and Yemeni Arabic as L2 targets** — zero acquisition
   studies (Yemeni appears in SLA only as learners' L1). Egyptian fares
   slightly better (Trentman; Huntley's stimuli model Egyptian variation).
3. **Chunk inventories and chunk-instruction for any Arabic dialect** — the
   only FS list is MSA; we must derive dialect chunks from our own corpora.
4. **Shadowing with dialectal Arabic** — no studies; durability of shadowing
   gains is unverified even for English.
5. **Hours-to-conversational-proficiency for self-directed app learners** —
   the only timelines are FSI/DLI institutional figures; DLI's 16-week
   conversion allocation is the nearest anchor.
6. **Arabic utterance-fluency norms and pause-location coding conventions**
   — thresholds must be calibrated internally.
7. **App/SRS-based dialect learning efficacy** — no controlled study of any
   app teaching a spoken Arabic dialect.
8. **Output-hypothesis mechanics in Arabic** — the one direct test (Nassif
   2019, beginners) found no unique noticing advantage for output; salience
   mattered more. Design pushed-output tasks with built-in salience/feedback
   rather than assuming production alone does the work.

## 8. Summary verdict table

| Research claim | Verdict | Product consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Richards' five plateau characteristics | Confirmed as his synthesis; plateau construct itself empirically thin | Fine as a design frame; don't cite as established science |
| "Stuck at basic 2,000 words" | **Wrong number** — Richards says 3,000; thresholds are English-specific anyway | Don't port vocab-size thresholds to Arabic |
| Input alone insufficient; output has value | Supported-but-nuanced; mechanism contested; Arabic test partially null | Engineer output tasks (fixes Krashen's scarcity) with salience + feedback; claim opportunity, not proof |
| Fluency built on formulaic chunks | Supported (strong processing evidence); "100,000s of stems" unverified | Chunk-centric content justified; SRS-style repeated exposure over awareness-raising |
| Learners overuse safe phrases, underuse repertoire (teddy bear) | Confirmed (Ellis 2012) | Deliberately surface strong-association chunks beyond raw frequency |
| Shadowing works | Fluency/comprehensibility supported; segmentals inconclusive; durability unverified | Score shadowing on fluency/prosody, not phonemes; ~5 reps/passage default |
| 2,200 hours for Arabic | Confirmed FSI origin, single-source, MSA-inclusive | Frame dialect goal as smaller unmeasured target; DLI 16-wk conversion is the anchor |
| MSA-first helps speaking | No supporting study; convergent evidence for integration/dialect-first | Validates the app's dialect-only premise (itself unstudied) |
| 3–5 min monologue samples | Unverified inference; 1–2 min carries most signal; multiple tasks > one long one | Scale length with level; prefer several short prompts for reliable composites |
